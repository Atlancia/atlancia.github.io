Difference Needs Somewhere to Go

Liam Shaw September 30, 2026

I was raised up believing I was somehow unique. Like a snowflake distinct among snowflakes. Unique in each way you'd conceive. And now after some thinking I'd say I'd rather be, a functioning cog in some great machinery, serving something beyond me.

Opening lyrics to Helplessness Blues by Fleet Foxes

At about twenty-five, I was in my first real job, working on a major project. We were on the cusp of a complex, multi-million-dollar initiative, about to scale from a business-case team of five into a delivery operation of more than fifty. On my way to work every day, I listened to music on a ten-minute walk. I rarely got through more than the first few tracks of an album before arriving, saying hello, and starting my day.

When Helplessness Blues came out, the title track stuck with me. I had never heard "being a cog in a machine" used as anything other than an insult, but here it was presented as a relief. In the right machine, you could contribute to something far better than anything you could build alone. The idea of being a unique snowflake as an exhausting, solitary burden had never crossed my mind before.

As our project grew, my place on a small team where relationships were tight and personal came to an end. On that small team, we had the time to see each other as people. We knew our quirks, and could see where a colleague's former experience in finance, a talent for trivia, or a knack for storytelling was far more valuable than any job description could capture.

Moving from that tight-knit group to my first large-scale project brought an anxious transition. I did not know where I was supposed to fit, or what cog I was supposed to be in what machine. I could feel a shift in the room from a dynamic where the right answer emerged from discussion to one where the right answer came from hierarchy. I was unsure where our differences fit when truth was no longer found through debate, but dictated by a spot on an organizational chart.

Throughout my career, that tension kept returning: When to bring more of myself into the room. When to make space for someone else. How to contribute to something larger without assuming that contribution requires leaving individuality at the door.

Having been away from work for a while, I find myself preparing for interviews and realizing I cannot shake the habit of saying “we.”

When I talk about work I am proud of, I talk about what we built, what we changed, and what we figured out. The predictable next question from an interviewer is always: Yes, but what did you do?

Part of that habit is an aversion to self-promotion. But mostly, claiming individual credit for a complex outcome simply is not what happened. That is an experience I miss, and understanding what made it possible has become a benchmark for what I want to build next.

None of Us Knows Enough

Years later, working as a Scrum Master, I started reading research on effective teams and recognized something I had already lived.

What struck me was not how novel it sounded, but how familiar it felt: a shared goal, close interaction, an intuitive sense of what each person could bring, and the ability to work out an answer together. Those qualities had defined our small business-case team years earlier, long before I had the vocabulary for them.

The teams I served that worked best understood a premise (implicit or explicitly) that was comforting and simple:

None of us knows enough.

These were cross-functional teams, bringing different kinds of expertise to a shared problem. But what mattered was not just the mix of people. It was that the work required their contributions to affect one another. Assembled because the problem required knowledge that no single individual, and often no single discipline, could supply.

Difference was not an administrative nuisance to contain; it was the reason to assemble the team. It was not about a developer who failed to understand business, a designer who ignored engineering constraints, or a specialist who arrived after the interesting work was finished just to test what had been built. It was about what those different perspectives could build together.

Often the most valuable talent was an ability to notice what the rest of us missed.

Their background, their judgment, or their way of seeing a problem could fundamentally alter what the team thought it was doing. An operational constraint might reshape the design. The revised design might eliminate an expensive technical dependency. A business question might reveal that we were solving the wrong problem entirely.

The value was in those contributions colliding while it was still easy to change direction.

Shared Understanding, Not Shared Sameness

Organizational literature often talks about "shared mental models" as the foundation of team coordination. Do we understand what we are trying to achieve, how the moving pieces fit together, and what we can reasonably expect from one another?

On a healthy team, knowing that a colleague once worked in a different role is more than an interesting fact. It changes who we consult, what risks we anticipate, and how we approach new problems. Each person brings more than their title, and if the group understands enough about each member they can put that depth to work. Shared understanding does not mean everyone knows the exact same things; it means our different skills have somewhere functional to meet.

It also means no single leader needs to hold the entire answer. A manager can hold responsibility for overall direction without pretending to have the domain depth to resolve every trade-off alone. The team ought to take collective responsibility for coordinating activities, solving problems, and evaluating how it operates.

This model is not just better in the moment; it is resilient over time. The UK government’s doctrine on organizational resilience captures this when defining what a common purpose in a resilient organization should look like: leaders must "communicate and reinforce the importance of collaboration and a 'beyond silos' mindset in working towards the achievement of the organisation’s essential outcomes."

At team level, when we understand what we are trying to accomplish and what each person can contribute, we have a basis for changing how we work when circumstances change. If we only understand our narrow, assigned lanes, we can produce our best work for a solution long after it has stopped making sense.

Shared understanding includes how we expect the work to happen, not just what we produce. And committing to that inevitably leads to conversations nobody particularly wants to have.

The Compromise Cannot Be One-Way

I once worked with a team where the default instinct was private incubation. Colleagues preferred to keep their ideas close, spend time developing them independently, and bring them to the table once they felt complete.

My instinct is the exact opposite. I rarely know what to fully think about a problem until we can put an idea on the board, test it through debate, pull it apart, and discard it without hurt feelings if the logic is weak.

To me, bringing a rough idea early was an act of collaboration. To them, it felt like reckless noise. Worse, questioning an idea I had held for ten minutes felt to me like open dialogue; questioning an idea they had worked on for three days felt like a challenge to their competence. By the time their concepts reached the room, days of personal effort had already been invested. Scrutinizing the premise meant asking someone to scrap a week of work. The room grew defensive. We were negotiating around finished, hardened positions when we could have been shaping unfinished thoughts together.

I had to examine my own costs, too. A fast, confident explanation can consume all the oxygen in a room before someone else has even formed their thought. What I experienced as open exploration could land on others as an aggressive, settled position.

Yet, over time, the expectation of adjustment became a one-way street. An implied moral hierarchy emerged: quiet hesitation was treated as inherently thoughtful and humble, while a desire for direct engagement was treated as an abrasive habit that needed to be tempered.

Slowing down was a reasonable request, but it was only half an answer. Without getting engagement back, alternative concepts, and active debate we had no shared mental model. We just had individuals waiting their turn to speak and not engaging with the ideas of their peers. Meetings felt like they could have been an email.

Eventually, I addressed the dynamic directly:

“I am willing to take leaps toward your style, but I need you to take steps toward mine.”

Not every thought had to be spoken immediately. But we needed enough exchange that silence did not leave us guessing whether people were reflecting, disagreeing, or waiting for their turn to share a disconnected piece of work.

It was uncomfortable, but it worked. Colleagues began bringing rough thoughts to the table. They challenged my proposals in real time, filling the pauses that I had previously rushed to fill with repetitive explanations. We had more room to alter direction before changing an idea meant undoing days of private effort.

Pocket Ready Rules

While I have spent hours stewing over these thoughts, in a few weeks, the details will fade. However, a few punchy phrases, a repeatable mantra or a punchy quote have the stickiness to be there when needed.

As I look ahead at the future, the environments I want to build, and the teams, products, and projects I want to take on, these are the sticky lines stand out through this reflection:

  • Difference needs somewhere to go.
    It will always exist, but without constructive channel, it settles into quiet cynicism, hallway complaints, and defensive silos. Brought into the open early, difference is a benefit. A room without friction isn't aligned; it is just quiet
  • Nobody knows enough.
    In work that is novel, complex and requires learning to do, the goal is never to be the smartest person at the table, but to ensure the table is smarter than any one person sitting at it. If one competency can solve a problem alone, the rest of the room is expensive overhead. In cross-functional teams we are not assembled to protect narrow jurisdictions or complete parallel tasks in isolation.
  • Truth has to come from debate, not an organizational chart.
    Hierarchy can allocate a budget, set a broad mandate, and carry the final accountability, but it cannot manufacture insight. Good environments are ones where arguments are tested on their merits, where half-formed thinking is invited in while the clay is wet, and where challenging an assumption is recognized as engagement not insubordination.
  • New information is never an inconvenient penalty
    Discovering a flaw in the plan should not feel like an attack on someone's competence or a burden that ruins days of work. It is what makes the result resilient. Spotting a design flaw or a missed constraint early in a project is not wasted effort; it is an early escape from an expensive failure. Discovering an error is simply the price of making contact with reality.
  • The most rewarding work is work where “we” is not a polite substitution for “I.”
    It is an accurate description of how the outcome came to exist: not one strong individual with a supporting cast, and not a group of isolated contributors executing assignments in separate lanes. It is a group producing something that required what each person brought, where those contributions changed one another, and where the answer could never have existed in pieces.

Managing genuine difference is uncomfortable. It requires us to sit in the tension of the unresolved without rushing to force an artificial consensus. It asks us to slow down our tempo to let a colleague form a thought, while simultaneously expecting more methodical thinkers to bring their ideas forward before it is too late to change direction.

I have come to accept that regardless of formal title, I cannot simply tuck my own differences away and remain a passive participant. It shows up in picking up the whiteboard marker first to give an ambiguous problem a shape people can respond to, or physically rearranging the tables and chairs before a meeting so nobody is stranded outside the circle.

For those of us who share that desire to leave impacts where we go, we cannot disconnect from it for long. Finding, building and holding onto the rewarding environments that produce the best work will require coming back to how they emerged and the craft of curating our best ideas and practice will continue.
